The ranking is a tool for defining the p-value, not a description of how likely each p-value is

Ranking answers: "for one specific study, what number do we assign it?" Probability answers: "if I run many studies, how often does each assigned number show up?" The first is a labeling rule. The second is the histogram.

Step 1: the labeling rule (ranking)

Take all the null-world studies and line them up from most extreme to least extreme. Label each study with its rank divided by the total. The most extreme study gets a label near 0, the most ordinary gets a label near 1. Those labels are the p-values.

Step 2: the probability question (the histogram)

Now ask: how often does each label occur? Count how many studies got a label between 0.04 and 0.05, and how many got one between 0.94 and 0.95. Each rank slot is used exactly once, so a label range of width 0.01 always contains 1% of the studies. That is why the histogram is flat.

So the flatness is a consequence of how the labels were built. Each study gets one label, labels are handed out evenly from 0 to 1, and therefore every stretch of the label scale holds the same share of studies.

A classroom analogy

Say 100 students take an exam, and you label each by class standing: 1st, 2nd, ..., 100th. Now ask, "what is the probability that a randomly chosen student has standing between 41st and 50th?" It's 10%. "Between 91st and 100th?" Also 10%. The standings are not "likely" or "unlikely" on their own. They are just evenly handed out. The exam scores might clump around 75, but that clumping doesn't change how standings are distributed.

Replace "exam score" with "t statistic" and "class standing" with "p-value," and you have the same situation.

Where the true "probability" content lives

The meaning of a particular p-value is a probability statement: p = 0.03 says "3% of null-world studies are at least this extreme." That is a probability about the t statistic. The flat histogram is a different statement, about how the p-values themselves are spread. It is a probability statement about the p-value as a random quantity.

So there are two levels:

Level 1, about t: P(|T| ≥ |t_obs|) = p. This is the definition.
Level 2, about p: P(p ≤ c) = c. This follows from Level 1 applied to every study, and it is the flat histogram.

Level 2 is just Level 1 turned around: if "p = c" means "the top c fraction of studies are at least this extreme," then the fraction of studies with p ≤ c is, by definition, c.
